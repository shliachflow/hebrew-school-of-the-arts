What the Morning Looks Like

Two and a half hours. Nothing wasted.

Parents always ask what actually happens. Here it is, start to finish.

  1. 10:00

    Arrival & good morning

    Coats off, aprons on. We start the morning together and take a look at the week ahead.

  2. 10:15

    Hebrew

    Small groups by reading level, not by age. Every child moves at their own pace — from first letters all the way to reading full prayers.

  3. 10:50

    Kitchen or studio

    Divisions rotate. Half the school is in the kitchen; half is in the art studio. Next week they switch.

  4. 11:40

    Torah & story

    The week's Torah portion and the people in it, told by teachers who know how to stop on a cliffhanger.

  5. 12:15

    Taste & pickup

    We eat what we made. Whatever doesn't get eaten goes home in a container with your child's name on it.

Every division follows this rhythm, adapted to age. The youngest get more movement and more snack. The teen divisions get more depth, more responsibility, and more freedom in the kitchen.

Sprouts · Ages 3–5

Their first taste of everything.

The beginning of a Jewish life, and it should be delicious. Sprouts runs on songs, snacks, mess and repetition. They'll know their Hebrew letters, the holidays, and the smell of a Friday kitchen before they can write their own name.

  1. First Hebrew letters through song, movement, and play
  2. Simple hands-on kitchen projects — mixing, rolling, decorating
  3. Art, sensory stations, and story time
  4. Holiday celebrations they'll never forget

Roots · Ages 6–8

Where it all starts to click.

The years the foundation gets built. Roots kids learn to read Hebrew, learn the story of our people, and spend real time in the kitchen and the studio — because a child who makes the holiday remembers the holiday.

  1. Hebrew reading in small leveled groups
  2. Full kitchen rotations — challah, latkes, hamantaschen, matzah
  3. Art studio projects tied to the Jewish year
  4. The stories, heroes, and holidays of a Jewish childhood

The Mitzvah Crew · Boys, Ages 9–13

Real fire. Real knives. Real responsibility.

The boys' division is built on something eleven-year-old boys almost never get: actual trust. They run their own stations in a working kitchen, with real equipment and real heat. They take on real Torah learning. And they reach their Bar Mitzvah having done a great deal more than memorize a reading.

  1. Full run of the kitchen — real equipment, real recipes
  2. Torah, an introduction to Talmud, and the questions boys this age actually ask
  3. Tefillin, and what it means to grow up Jewish
  4. Bar Mitzvah preparation and one-on-one coaching

The Bat Mitzvah Club · Girls, Ages 9–13

Becoming the woman she's meant to be.

Her own program, her own space, her own mentors. Part learning, part kitchen, part art — and part something harder to put on a website: a room full of girls who are proud of who they are, guided by women they look up to.

  1. What it means to grow into a Jewish woman, and the traditions that are hers
  2. Culinary workshops and dessert design in the kitchen
  3. Creative projects, journaling, and the art studio
  4. Bat Mitzvah preparation and personal mentorship

Hebrew Reading

Ten levels. They know exactly which one they're on.

We teach reading with Aleph Champ, which works the way karate belts do. Every child starts where they actually are and moves up one color at a time. A beginner is never behind, an advanced reader is never bored, and progress is something a seven-year-old can see.

Beyond the Classroom

A real kitchen. Not a hot plate in a classroom.

Every division rotates through the kitchen every month. They measure, they knead, they burn a batch and make it again.

  • Rosh HashanahChallah, braided six different ways
  • HanukkahLatkes and jelly doughnuts, from scratch
  • PurimA hamantaschen production line
  • PassoverMatzah, eighteen minutes, hands covered in flour
  • ShavuotCheesecake, obviously

Whatever they make, they take home.

The Art Room

The “Arts” was never decoration.

There has been a dedicated art teacher here since the beginning, and that room is the reason a lot of kids show up. Mosaics, painting, sculpture, ceramics, mixed media — always tied to what they're learning.

They don't take home a worksheet. They take home an heirloom.

A menorah they designed themselves. A seder plate their family will use for twenty years.
